The IELTS Speaking test is a conversation with an examiner in three parts. It lasts 11 to 14 minutes and is assessed on four things: fluency and coherence, lexical resource (vocabulary), grammatical range and accuracy, and pronunciation.

Many candidates lose marks not because their English is weak, but because of habits that hide what they can do. Here are six of the most common ones, and what to do instead.

1. Giving one-word or very short answers

In Part 1 the questions are simple, so it's tempting to answer simply. But a very short answer gives the examiner almost nothing to assess.

Too shortQ: Do you like cooking?
"Yes, I do."
BetterQ: Do you like cooking?
"Yes, quite a lot, especially at weekends when I have more time. I usually try one new recipe, though it doesn't always go to plan!"

Aim for two or three sentences: answer, then add a reason, an example or a detail.

2. Using memorised answers

Examiners are trained to recognise rehearsed answers. They often sound unnatural and may not quite match the question. Memorised language can also be marked down because it doesn't show what you can do spontaneously.

Instead of memorising answers, prepare ideas and vocabulary for common topics like work, study, hometown, hobbies and technology, then practise speaking about them in different ways.

3. Stopping when you don't know a word

Long silences hurt your fluency score more than a simple word does. If you can't find the exact word, describe it, a skill called paraphrasing.

Paraphrasing in action "We used a… I'm not sure of the exact word, but it's a kind of large pan you use for cooking rice over a fire."

4. Not using the full two minutes in Part 2

In Part 2 you get a task card, one minute to prepare, and then you speak for up to two minutes. Many candidates stop after 40 or 50 seconds. Use your preparation minute to write keywords for each bullet point on the card, not full sentences, and add a personal story or feeling to extend your talk naturally.

5. Over-correcting every small mistake

Correcting yourself occasionally is natural. But stopping to fix every small error breaks your flow and can make you sound less fluent. If you notice a mistake, correct it quickly and keep going. If you're not sure, just carry on.

6. Giving simple opinions in Part 3

Part 3 asks more abstract questions, often about society, change or the future. Answers like "I think it's good" are too thin. Show that you can discuss ideas: give a view, explain why, consider another side, and reach a conclusion.

A useful pattern for Part 3 View: "I'd say it's mostly positive…" → Reason: "…because…" → Other side: "Having said that, some people might argue…" → Conclusion: "So overall…"

Quick checklist before test day

  • Can you answer Part 1 questions in two or three natural sentences?
  • Can you talk for close to two minutes from keywords?
  • Do you have phrases ready for buying time, like "That's an interesting question…"?
  • Have you practised discussing both sides of an issue?
